# BenefitFlow Research Artifact — Privacy / Consent / Regulatory Baseline v0.1

**Project:** BenefitFlow  
**Lane:** privacy-consent-regulatory  
**Researcher:** researcher-5e9c  
**Date:** 2026-10-03  
**Status:** Initial evidence baseline; requires primary/reviewer disposition and legal review before production use.

## Executive finding

BenefitFlow’s current product boundary is directionally sound: it stops before a live transaction, masks identifiers in user-facing proposal output, requires provider verification before proposal creation, and requires user approval before a transaction-adapter handoff. However, a production system handling extended-benefits, provider, insurance, appointment, and health-adjacent data needs a first-class privacy/consent control plane rather than treating approval as a single boolean.

The highest-priority design change is to separate **workflow approval** from **privacy authorization**. A user saying “book this” should not be represented as an unlimited consent to collect, retain, or disclose every identifier in the account. Production disclosure should be bound to an exact purpose, recipient, field set, transaction, time window, and revocation/expiry state.

## Current Canadian legal baseline (2026-10-03)

### Federal

PIPEDA remains Canada’s operative federal private-sector privacy law for covered commercial activity. The former Bill C-27 did not become law in the prior Parliament. A new federal reform proposal, Bill C-36 (Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act), was introduced June 15, 2026 and is currently at second reading; it should be treated as a horizon item, not as current law.

For PIPEDA-covered activity:
- collection, use, and disclosure require an appropriate purpose and generally meaningful consent;
- sensitive information generally calls for express consent;
- collection should be limited to what is necessary for the identified purpose;
- organizations remain accountable for information transferred to third-party processors and must use contractual or other means to provide comparable protection;
- health and financial information are expressly treated as highly sensitive examples for safeguards;
- breach records must be kept, and breaches creating a real risk of significant harm trigger reporting to the OPC and notification to affected individuals.

The OPC’s September 2026 third-party-provider guidance is especially relevant to BenefitFlow. It reinforces pre-contract due diligence, jurisdiction/subprocessor analysis, data-flow understanding, contractual protections, and ongoing accountability. The guidance is currently open for comments until December 4, 2026, so implementation should track any final revisions.

### Provincial private-sector laws

British Columbia, Alberta, and Quebec have private-sector privacy statutes that operate alongside the federal framework depending on the activity and jurisdiction.

- **British Columbia:** PIPA regulates private-sector organizations that collect, use, or disclose personal information. BenefitFlow should assume BC PIPA obligations for BC-local commercial handling unless counsel determines a different jurisdictional allocation.
- **Alberta:** PIPA is Alberta’s private-sector privacy law. Separately, Alberta’s Health Information Act applies to health custodians and can extend operational duties to affiliates, including persons performing services for custodians under contract or agency relationships. If BenefitFlow integrates directly with regulated providers, the role classification must be reviewed.
- **Quebec:** Quebec’s private-sector framework places strong emphasis on necessity, valid consent, governance, and privacy impact assessment. Before transferring or outsourcing personal information outside Quebec, the enterprise must complete a privacy impact assessment and use a written agreement; the enterprise remains responsible for the information. Sensitive-information handling and new purposes require heightened consent discipline.
- **Ontario:** PHIPA governs personal health information in the health-care ecosystem. If BenefitFlow merely acts for a consumer, its classification may differ from a product acting as an agent/service provider for a health information custodian. Ontario law also contains a “consumer electronic service provider” concept for services requested by individuals to access/use/manage their personal health information. Production integration mode therefore matters: consumer tool, custodian agent, and provider-facing integration should be treated as distinct compliance modes.

## Product invariants recommended for production

1. **Purpose before collection.** Every sensitive field must have a declared purpose before collection. Consent does not cure unnecessary collection.
2. **Granular privacy authorization.** Persist a `ConsentGrant`/`DisclosureAuthorization` distinct from booking approval. Bind it to:
   - purpose;
   - recipient identity and recipient type;
   - exact fields/categories authorized;
   - transaction/proposal ID;
   - consent text/version shown to the user;
   - timestamp;
   - expiry;
   - withdrawal status;
   - jurisdiction/context.
3. **Least disclosure by default.** The transaction adapter should receive only the minimum data required for the verified step. Full member/plan identifiers should not be included in general workflow state.
4. **Sensitive-data segregation.** Put raw insurance/member identifiers and uploaded plan records behind a dedicated secret/data-vault interface with encryption, scoped access, audit, TTL/retention, and deletion controls. General workflow SQLite/JSON state should hold references or masked values only.
5. **Processor accountability.** Maintain a machine-readable register of processors/subprocessors, data categories, purpose, region/jurisdiction, contract status, breach-notice terms, and review date.
6. **Retention is explicit.** Add record-class retention rules for plan documents, parsed benefits, provider verification, consent records, transaction artifacts, call transcripts/audio (if later added), and identifiers.
7. **Access/correction/export/delete workflows.** Build user-facing privacy rights workflows rather than relying on administrator database access.
8. **Breach event model.** Security/privacy incidents require durable incident records, risk assessment, affected data classes, jurisdiction, notification decision, and evidence of containment.
9. **Jurisdiction-aware policy engine.** Store user/transaction jurisdiction separately from free-form address data and apply privacy/consent requirements at the action boundary.
10. **No secondary model training by default.** Personal benefit/health/financial information should not become training data for internal or third-party models unless a separately justified, disclosed, and authorized governance path exists.
11. **Separate research from production PII.** Research agents should receive sanitized/de-identified test inputs only. Production member/plan identifiers should remain outside researcher context.
12. **Transaction-scoped external disclosure.** Approval should authorize only the verified provider/service/price/window and the minimum identifiers required for that transaction. Material changes require fresh approval and, where disclosure scope changes, fresh privacy authorization.

## Code observations against current Beta v0.3.0

### 1. Raw identifiers are persisted in general-purpose SQLite state

`benefitflow_beta/workflow.py:create_proposal()` stores the full `plan_number` and `member_id` under a `sensitive` object. `benefitflow_beta/storage.py` serializes all values into a generic SQLite `kv` table as JSON.

That is acceptable for a synthetic/local beta boundary but is a production blocker for real member data because the storage layer currently has no encryption-at-rest abstraction, per-field access control, purpose binding, TTL/retention enforcement, or security audit trail.

**Recommendation:** replace direct persistence of raw identifiers with a `SensitiveValueRef` pointing to a dedicated secure storage adapter. Keep only masked tails and opaque handles in workflow state.

### 2. Booking approval is not a sufficient privacy consent record

`BookingApprovalRequest` contains only `proposal_id` and `approved: bool`. There is no durable record of exactly which information the user authorized BenefitFlow to disclose, to whom, for what purpose, under which notice/consent language, or for how long.

**Recommendation:** add separate immutable `ConsentGrant` and `DisclosureEvent` models. A transaction adapter must validate an active grant before retrieving a secret.

### 3. Transaction handoff language is safety-aware but not mechanically enforceable

`approve_proposal()` correctly instructs the future adapter to reconfirm the clinic’s need for identifiers, not accept materially different terms, and avoid payments/claims without separate authorization. These are good policy constraints, but they currently exist as generated prose.

**Recommendation:** represent these limits as structured adapter policy:
- allowed recipient/provider ID;
- allowed service/category;
- allowed maximum price;
- allowed appointment window;
- allowed disclosure fields;
- prohibited actions;
- expiry;
- reapproval triggers.

The script should be a rendering of machine-enforced policy, not the policy itself.

### 4. No privacy-rights or retention layer exists

The current key/value store exposes `put` and `get` only. There is no first-class delete, retention expiry, export, correction, or provenance/access-log function.

**Recommendation:** add a privacy data service before any real user data is accepted.

## Engineering work items for Primary/Reviewer triage

### P0 — before real personal data
- `PRIV-001`: Consent/disclosure authorization ledger separate from booking approval.
- `PRIV-002`: Secure secret-store abstraction; remove raw plan/member identifiers from generic workflow state.
- `PRIV-003`: Structured transaction policy that mechanically constrains recipient, fields, purpose, value limits, and expiry.
- `PRIV-004`: Data inventory + purpose registry + processor/subprocessor registry.
- `PRIV-005`: Retention/deletion and user access/correction/export capability.
- `PRIV-006`: Security/privacy event audit log and breach workflow.

### P1 — before insurer/provider integrations
- `PRIV-101`: Jurisdiction/compliance-mode resolver (consumer-only vs provider/custodian integration).
- `PRIV-102`: Third-party privacy/security due-diligence checklist and contract metadata.
- `PRIV-103`: Quebec outside-Quebec processing PIA gate and written-agreement evidence.
- `PRIV-104`: Ontario PHIPA integration classification review for custodian agent/service-provider and consumer-electronic-service-provider scenarios.
- `PRIV-105`: Alberta HIA role review for integrations performed under contract/agency with custodians.

### P2 — before voice automation or call recording
- `PRIV-201`: Separate call-recording/transcription consent, retention, redaction, and provider-notice controls.
- `PRIV-202`: Prevent voice/transcript data from entering model-training pipelines by default.

## Open legal/product questions

1. What is BenefitFlow’s intended launch geography: BC-only, selected provinces, or Canada-wide?
2. Will BenefitFlow contract directly with clinics/providers, or act only at the consumer’s instruction?
3. Will insurers provide APIs or portal integrations that make BenefitFlow a processor/service provider under insurer contracts?
4. Will production store source plan PDFs, claims history, diagnosis/treatment information, prescriptions/referrals, or only benefit entitlement data?
5. Will calls be recorded/transcribed, and if so, where will audio/transcripts be processed?
6. What cloud regions/subprocessors are intended?
7. Will minors/dependants be supported? If yes, substitute decision-maker/guardian and age-specific consent flows need their own workstream.

## Researcher disposition

**Finding:** The beta is safe enough to continue synthetic R&D, but production ingestion of real benefit/member data should be gated on PRIV-001 through PRIV-006. The most important architectural change is a privacy authorization layer that is independent from generic workflow approval.
